Letters times letters

Lately I've been thinking about my university projects. If someone asked me what I find beautiful about data science, this is the one I'd tell them about. I've always said AI is really good, but it won't replace the creativity that some parts of the work need. This project is why.

At the end of 2024, for a university class, we got a public dataset of workers' compensation claims from New York. The goal was to predict how each claim would end. There were eight possible outcomes, from the claim being cancelled, to only medical costs being covered, to permanent disability, to death. The model wasn't performing the way we wanted, so a friend and I went back to the data. We had where each person got hurt, an arm, a leg, the head, the neck. And we had the type of injury, a sprain, a fracture, an amputation. On their own, those columns were just labels. The model could tell them apart, but it had no idea that losing an arm is worse than spraining an ankle. We kept saying there had to be something we could do with them.

So we tried to turn them into a new feature, a column the model learns from. First we grouped the body parts into regions, and gave each region a weight taken from a 2019 study on scoring trauma injuries, where the head and the neck weigh the most. Then we grouped the types of injury on a scale from minor to fatal, following the Abbreviated Injury Scale, a standard way of rating how bad an injury is. Then we multiplied the two and called the result an accident severity score. On paper the formula is just letters times letters, W times S, a body region weight times a severity score. But it makes sense the moment you read it. A fracture to the head should count for more than a fracture to a finger, and the score says exactly that.

We checked that it behaved the way it should, with deaths and permanent disabilities scoring highest, and that the weights weren't set in a ridiculous way.

It helped, but less than I remembered. When we ranked all the features, it landed somewhere in the middle. The ones that carried the model were how much people earned, how many independent medical exams they had, and whether they had a lawyer. In the end we kept the pieces the score was built from, not the score itself. That doesn't take away from it for me. We had taken two columns of labels and turned them into a number that meant something. I still find that pretty amazing. If you really go and investigate data, you can take so much out of it.

Back then I used AI for one small part. I asked it to write the lists for the groupings, because I didn't want to sort every type of injury by hand. Even that needed correcting. The tools have changed a lot since then. If I built the score today, I'd hand the model much more. The searching online, the medical background (I'm not medically trained), the lists and most of the code. I'd still read the Python, because I'm used to it and it's fun, but I wouldn't write it myself like I did then.

What I'd keep is the idea of the score, how it works, and validating it. I used to say AI won't replace this. Now I'd put it differently. It's not that it can't. I'd just rather it didn't.

Say the model built the score and then walked me through every step. In my experience that isn't the same. To really follow a project someone else built, you have to go through all of it, and by then you've almost done it yourself, except you never spend as much time on it. You lose the thread of where the data comes from and how the feature is built. And when a feature does end up mattering, I want to be able to explain where it came from.

There's also something simpler. In my experience, building a feature like this by hand is fun, and it has what I'd call a kind of poetry. Someone starting out today who never does it could miss that. They could also miss learning how far feature engineering can go beyond the original data while still being related to what you're trying to predict.

So the way I try to use it is to build with AI, not let AI build it for me. Used like that, I can still understand the feature, its impact, and why it has that impact.
